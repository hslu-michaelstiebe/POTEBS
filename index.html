POTEBS.

Investigating the Potential of E-Bike-Sharing Systems for Sustainable Mobility in Different Spatial Types.

Linking 1,844 survey responses to 1.4 million operational trip records across two concurrently operating systems in Basel, POTEBS identifies what separates power users from dormant registrants — and challenges common assumptions about what drives intensive EBSS use.

Keywords e-bike sharing · usage intensity · user segmentation · ordered logit · multimodal mobility · Basel

Core finding · published 2026

Electric bike sharing is useful in a real but bounded way.

Intensive EBSS use is not an expression of environmental ideology. It emerges where shared e-bikes solve concrete mobility problems better than the available alternatives — above all in combination with public transport.

— POTEBS · HSLU · Basel

Findings

What the results show.

Pragmatic utility, not environmental identity, explains who rides intensively (combined ordered logit: McFadden pseudo-R²=0.104, N=1,743).

I.
Usage concentration

Extreme concentration and near-50% dormancy.

39% Medium
13% Power
0.20 rentals / month 1.60 rentals / month 12.71 rentals / month
Gini 0.77 · Top 20% generate 80.3% of trips

Three segments from Gaussian Mixture Modeling on rentals per month. Usage is more concentrated in the dock-based system (Gini 0.83 vs 0.75), where power users are 2.4 times as common (23.5% vs 9.6%).

II.
The environmental paradox

Pro-environmental self-identity reduces the odds of intensive use.

−18% OR 0.82 · p=0.01 Environmental self-identity → usage intensity

The opposite of what the Theory of Planned Behavior predicts. In Basel, green-minded users may simply walk, cycle or take the tram instead.

III.
What actually drives intensity

Multimodal integration lifts it, reliability doubts suppress it.

+53% OR 1.53 · p<0.001 Multimodal complementarity (F5)
−39% OR 0.61 · p<0.001 Service reliability & cost concerns (F6)

Using EBSS alongside public transport, and as a fallback when the network fails, is the strongest driver. Doubts about availability and cost are the strongest brake.

IV.
Two archetypes

Routine optimizers and flexibility maximizers.

+53% OR 1.53 · p=0.002 Dock-based · perceived superiority over PT (PubliBike)
+74% OR 1.74 · p<0.001 Free-floating · multimodal complementarity (Pick-e-Bike)

Dock-based routine optimizers are urban, highly educated commuters on fixed corridors where the bike beats the tram. Free-floating flexibility maximizers are younger, less often own a car (48% vs 69%), and use EBSS as one tool among several. Neither is driven by environmental ideology.

V.
Mode substitution

EBSS primarily substitutes public transport, not cars.

Provider → mode substitution. Flow width = share of users who substitute the mode at least sometimes (published Table 3; FFEBSS n=1,382, DBEBSS n=462).
Three-quarters of displaced trips come from already-sustainable modes

75.5% of users substitute public transport and 50.9% walking; only 33.9% of those with car access substitute car trips (free-floating 36.4%, dock-based 22.0%). EBSS works as a PT overflow valve and last-mile bridge, not as a car replacement.

The incubator question

Private e-bike ownership goes with lower shared use (OR=0.73, p<0.01). About half of the 629 owners and prospective buyers say EBSS contributed to that decision, more so among power users (63% vs 46%). Plausible, but the cross-sectional design cannot show causation.

The strongest positive predictor is multimodal complementarity (OR 1.53): users who combine EBSS with public transport and walking ride far more intensively. The strongest brake is reliability and cost concerns (OR 0.61). Pro-environmental self-identity is significant and negative (OR 0.82): more green-minded users are 18% less likely to sit in a higher-intensity segment.
By system design

Provider-specific models separate the two archetypes. Perceived functional superiority over public transport drives intensity in the dock-based system only; multimodal complementarity drives it in the free-floating system only. Environmental self-identity is positive in neither.

Implications

What this means in practice.

EBSS is a useful but bounded tool. Invest in what keeps frequent riders riding, not in broad environmental appeal.

Integration

Connect EBSS into the multimodal system.

The strongest driver is use alongside public transport. Three levers: fares (combined EBSS and PT subscriptions), information (availability in journey planners such as the SBB app), and siting (stations at transfer points).

Service quality

Reliability protects the frequent-rider base.

Doubts about availability and cost are the strongest brake. Fleet sizing, rebalancing, real-time availability and short reservations (for example 15 minutes) target exactly those doubts.

Messaging

Utility, not sustainability.

Frequent riders are not the most environmentally minded. Time savings and good PT connections are the better message.

Evaluation

Rethink what counts as success.

Most substituted trips were already sustainable, so CO₂ savings are a weak yardstick. Credit peak-hour relief for public transport, last-mile links and the fallback role during disruptions.

From research to practice · U-Two field test

Fare integration is now being tested in the Basel region: the U-Two field test (FHNW with HSLU, BVB, BLT and TNW) integrates shared bikes and e-bikes into the U-Abo and GA. It runs through 2026.

Second paper

Two configurations, one city.

1.47 million trips, no survey data. Each service is read as a bundle: docking logic, vehicle class, speed, service area and tariff differ at once, so no single design dimension explains a contrast.

Usage

Broad casual layer, dense frequent core.

Free-floating: 26,744 active users, median trip 2.05 km, Gini 0.72. Dock-based: 6,519 users, 1.44 km, Gini 0.80, 93% of activity inside Basel-Stadt, and a younger, more gender-balanced base.

Space

Population-anchored versus job-anchored.

Free-floating follows residential density and fades with distance from the centre; dock-based follows job density. Matching window and perimeter narrows the distance ratio from 1.49 to 1.19, so the gap is part coverage, part real.

Weather

Rain hits the morning peak hardest.

The free-floating AM peak is the most rain-sensitive estimate in the panel (IRR 0.67 per mm) and temperature-blind. Dock-based demand tracks temperature all day. Commuter rain-resistance depends on the bundle.

Scale

Too small to replace transit.

Both systems together are about 0.24% of BVB boardings. Free-floating starts drift toward PT classes B and C (34% to 42%); dock-based concentrates on class A hubs at around 80%.

What this means for planning

Two systems in one city, one benchmark: that is the mistake. Treating «bike sharing» as one category under-rewards either the regional reach or the per-user intensity. A small PT share means system-wide substitution is unlikely, not that the two are complementary: operational records hold no trip purpose and no mode substitution.

Interpretation

A Gini coefficient of 0.77 describes extreme inequality — comparable in magnitude to national income distributions in some of the world's most unequal economies, here applied to trip generation. The typical registered user contributes almost nothing to system activity; a tiny minority drives almost everything.

The project What POTEBS asks, how it is built, who is involved, and where it stands.
Questions

What this project asks.

How can EBSS support the mobility transition as part of a flexible multimodal mobility system?
RQ 01
Which user groups use EBSS and in what multimodal contexts; what hampers use and what benefits do users report?
RQ 02
What business models for EBSS or EBSS–PT combinations could build on user needs?
RQ 03
How can EBSS contribute to sustainable mobility in urban and peri-urban regions, and which measures support this?
Background

Viability rests on a small core of riders.

Across the literature, 10 to 22% of registered users generate 50 to 81% of trips, while roughly half never return after signing up. Operating costs frequently exceed revenues, and willingness-to-pay is low. Which users ride intensively is therefore not a marginal question but the one that decides whether a system can carry itself.

The research gap

Adoption is well studied; intensity is not.

Prior work explains who signs up and what drives aggregate demand. It does not explain which personal characteristics make a registered user a frequent rider, nor how that interacts with system design. POTEBS addresses this by linking about 1.4 million operational trip records to individual survey responses across a free-floating and a dock-based system operating side by side.
